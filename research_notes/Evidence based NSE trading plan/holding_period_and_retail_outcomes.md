## 1. SEBI's "Analysis of Intraday Trading by Individuals in Equity Cash Segment": exact findings and later updates

### Takeaway
SEBI released the study on **24 July 2024**, not July 2023 as the brief says. It covers FY19, FY22 and FY23. It found that **71% of individual intraday traders in the equity cash segment had net losses in FY23**, up from 65% in FY19. The loss rate rose to **80% for those making more than 500 trades a year**, was 76% for under-30s and 81% for under-20s, and was lowest at 53% for over-60s. Loss-makers paid trading costs equal to **an extra 57% of their trading losses**, while profit-makers paid 19% of their trading profits. No later SEBI update on intraday trading in the cash segment turned up. SEBI's 2025 and 2026 follow-up studies cover derivatives only.

### Cited Findings
- **Release date and scope:** The study was released on 24 July 2024 by SEBI's Department of Economic and Policy Analysis. It used individual-level broker data for FY19, FY22 and FY23. The ₹5,371 figure is probably an average over all intraday traders, but this is unconfirmed.

### Inferences
- If costs add 57% on top of trading losses, then costs make up about 57/157 ≈ **36% of a typical loss-maker's total net loss**. Costs alone did not create the losses, but they made them roughly 1.6 times larger.
- Even the best-performing group, traders over 60, lost money in the majority (53%). No age or frequency bucket in this study had a majority of profitable intraday traders.
- The low FY23 average loss of ₹5,371 is consistent with a population of small accounts, like the ₹20,000 account in this brief. The relevant base rate for this trader is therefore roughly 7 in 10 losing, and roughly 8 in 10 losing if they trade more than about 2 times a day (500 trades a year).

### Gaps
- I could not open the primary PDF, so the full frequency-bucket table (for example, the loss rate for traders with fewer than 100 trades a year), loss rate by gender, and the average-loss definitions are unverified.
- I found **no later SEBI update** of the intraday cash-segment study through Oct 2026. SEBI's July 2025 and August 2026 studies are about derivatives. A dedicated search for a 2025–26 intraday update could not be run because the search budget was exhausted.

## 2. SEBI's F&O studies (2023 to 2026), the post-October-2024 measures, and whether F&O is feasible at ₹20,000 with ₹200 risk

### Takeaway
SEBI's studies show 88–93% of individual F&O traders losing money every year:
- **89%** in FY22 (study of Jan 2023)
- **93%** over FY22–FY24 (Sept 2024)
- about **91%** in FY25 (July 2025)
- **87.7%** in FY26 (study of 20 Aug 2026)

After the October 2024 measures and the 1 April 2026 STT increase, the number of participants and total losses both fell by about 18% in FY26. The average loss per trader rose to about ₹1.17 lakh. Options account for 92% of individual losses. **F&O is effectively not feasible for a ₹20,000 account risking ₹200:**
- One Nifty lot is 65 units, about ₹15 lakh notional at 23,053.
- ₹200 of risk equals **3.08 index or premium points**.
- Futures or option selling needs about ₹1.6–2.4 lakh of margin, 8 to 12 times the account.
- Round-trip charges on one option lot alone cost about 0.24–0.39R before slippage.

The sample covered all individual clients of the top 10 brokers, about 67% of individual F&O turnover. ### Inferences
These are my calculations, using Nifty at 23,053, a lot of 65, and the charges listed in section 3.
- **Futures:** One lot is about **₹14.98 lakh notional**, so ₹200 of risk is a **3.08-point** Nifty stop (0.013%), which is far inside a single minute's noise. The ₹1.6–2.4 lakh margin is 8 to 12 times the account. **Futures are impossible for this account.**
- **Option selling:** It needs the same margin, so it is also impossible.
- **Option buying:** ₹20,000 buys at most one lot priced up to about ₹307 per unit (₹20,000 / 65). A ₹200 risk limit means a stop 3.08 premium points away: 3.1% of a ₹100 premium, or 1.5% of a ₹200 premium, which is noise. The only way to keep risk at ₹200 is to buy options priced at or below about ₹3, where the whole premium is the risk. Those are lottery tickets.
- **Option charges:** Round-trip charges for one lot are about ₹48 at a ₹3 premium and ₹63 at ₹100, which is **0.24–0.39R per trade before slippage**. ₹40 of that is two ₹20 brokerage orders plus GST. The new 0.15% STT adds further cost.
- **Stock options:** Lots worth at least ₹5 lakh notional make ₹200 of risk an even smaller fraction of the position, so they are also unworkable.
- **Track record of the measures:** The October 2024 measures reduced participation and total losses, but did **not** move the share of losing traders much: 93% (FY24), 90.9% (FY25), 87.7% (FY26). The average loss per trader rose.
- On base rates alone (about 88% losers, options 92% of losses, option buyers about 10% profitable per Zerodha), F&O offers this account the worst odds of the three choices, even before the feasibility problem.

### Gaps
- None of the primary SEBI PDFs could be fetched. Detailed breakdowns from the 2026 study (by contracts traded, index vs stock, intraday vs expiry-day) are missing.

- I could not confirm whether the semi-annual review has changed the Nifty lot from 65 since January 2026. At 23,053, 65 units is just under ₹15 lakh.

## 3. Cost per trade and position sizing at ₹200 risk (intraday MIS, swing CNC/MTF, F&O)

### Takeaway
Measured in R, cost per trade equals **round-trip cost % divided by stop distance %**:
- **Intraday (MIS):** round trip about 0.106% of notional at Zerodha, so a 0.5% stop costs about **0.21R** and a 0.25% stop about 0.38R.
- **Swing (CNC):** round trip about 0.22% plus a fixed ₹15.34 depository (DP) charge, so a 5% stop costs about **0.12R**, falling to about 0.10–0.15R for 3–10% stops.
- **Margin trading facility (MTF):** about 0.56R on a ₹4,000 position, so it is uneconomic at this size.
- **Option buying:** about 0.24–0.39R.

At realistic trade counts, intraday costs about **₹850–1,700 a month (4–9% of the account)** against about **₹150–250 a month (about 1%)** for swing.

## 4. Evidence for swing and positional strategies in Indian equities after costs

### Takeaway
The best Indian evidence covers **medium-horizon cross-sectional momentum**, meaning stocks ranked on 6–12 month returns and rebalanced monthly or half-yearly:
- NSE's **Nifty200 Momentum 30** TRI returned **18.8% CAGR from April 2005 to June 2026, against 13.9% for the Nifty 200 TRI**.
- It came with much higher volatility (about 20.5% vs 13.2%) and severe drawdowns. A backtest shows a fall of about 70% in 2008. A fund document cites −34%, but for an unspecified period and possibly a hybrid index.

Other evidence:
- **The 52-week-high effect** has one recent, large Indian study in support.
- **Post-earnings drift (PEAD)** evidence is mixed.
- **Short-term (weekly) reversal** evidence is thin.
- **Technical breakout and moving-average rules** generally fail after costs in Indian tests.

Almost none of this evidence is at the "days to a couple of weeks" horizon of classic swing trading. All of it comes from portfolio-level, gross or institutional-cost backtests, not from small accounts paying 0.2% STT plus ₹15 DP per exit.

### Cited Findings
**Nifty200 Momentum 30 (NSE Indices)**
- **Method:** The index picks 30 stocks from the Nifty 200 by a normalised momentum score built from 6- and 12-month price returns adjusted for daily volatility. Weights are free-float market cap times the score. It is rebalanced semi-annually, in June and December. — [NSE Indices whitepaper, Sep 2020](https://www.niftyindices.com/docs/default-source/indices/nifty200-momentum-30/nifty200_momentum_30_index_whitepaper_sep_20.pdf)
- **Whitepaper backtest (Sep 2020):** Since April 2005 the index returned 18.6% CAGR against 12.7% for the Nifty 200. Annualised volatility was 20.5% against 13.2%, and the return/risk ratio 0.90 against 0.51. On daily rolling 5-year returns it beat the Nifty 200 in 98.3% of instances. These figures are provider marketing data from before 2020. — [NSE whitepaper](https://www.niftyindices.com/docs/default-source/indices/nifty200-momentum-30/nifty200_momentum_30_index_whitepaper_sep_20.pdf)
- **Long-run returns to June 2026:** The TRI returned 18.8% CAGR from April 2005 to June 2026, against 13.9% for the Nifty 200 TRI. The 1-year return to June 2026 was about −2.5%. — [HDFC NIFTY200 Momentum 30 Index Fund presentation, July 2026](https://files.hdfcfund.com/s3fs-public/Others/2026-07/HDFC%20NIFTY200%20Momentum%2030%20Index%20Fund_PPT%20(July%202026).pdf). An independent educational backtest reports a **−70.53% drawdown in 2007–08 with a 65-month recovery**. — [BacktestIndia](https://backtestindia.com/blog/momentum-investing-india-backtest)

**Academic momentum evidence for India (mixed on whether it survives costs)**
- **Maheshwari & Dhankar** (NSE, 1997–2013): momentum was profitable over the sample, not driven by calendar effects, with strong negative returns in May. — [SSRN 2785526](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2785526)
- **Sehgal & Balakrishnan:** strong short-term momentum and a moderately positive contrarian payoff on long-term past returns. — [IJFM summary](https://www.publishingindia.com/archive/ijfm/a-study-of-contrarian-and-momentum-profits-in-indian-stock-market)
- **NSE monthly-returns paper:** significant short-term momentum and long-term overreaction, but much of the potential momentum and contrarian profit may disappear after transaction costs. It cites **Michello & Chowdhary (2010)**, who found insignificant momentum profits in India. — [Publishing India paper](https://www.publishingindia.com/storage/PDFBrochures/2192.pdf)
- **Ansari & Khan (2012)**, "Momentum anomaly: Evidence from India", Managerial Finance 38(2):206-223. **52-week-high effect in India**
- **Raju** (more than 4,400 Indian stocks, October 2004 to August 2023): the 52-week-high effect is a distinct anomaly from standard academic momentum. It holds across weighting schemes, gives more stable alpha than academic momentum, and shows weaker long-term reversals. Quantpedia's indicative backtest is 20.39% a year at 13.68% volatility. — [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/an-analysis-of-52-weeks-high-effect-on-indian-stocks/); [SSRN 4587697 copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/694152012/SSRN-id4587697)
- **Original idea:** George & Hwang (2004) introduced 52-week-high momentum. An Indian follow-up on "physical momentum" builds on this literature. — [Tripurana, arXiv 2302.13245](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2302.13245)

**Post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD) in India (conflicting)**
- **IIM Bangalore study** (NSE stocks, 120-day window): post-earnings drift exists. — [IIMB repository](https://repository.iimb.ac.in/handle/2074/17935)
- **Study of 469 Indian companies** (December 2002 to December 2011): strong continuation in earnings and post-event abnormal returns. I saw it only via a mirror site. — [DOI 10.1177/0972262914564042](https://doi.org/10.1177/0972262914564042)
- **University of Nottingham dissertation** (S&P BSE 500, 665 events, FY2007–2012): the market is efficient for good news, but prices drift negatively for more than a week after bad news. — [Nottingham eprints](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/26569)
- **2021 study** (100 NSE firms, 2014–2018): the market is efficient around earnings with no excess returns, which refutes earlier evidence of inefficiency. The source link is uncertain; it surfaced as one of [ISBR journal](https://www.isbr.in/journals/52-64.pdf) or [IJMST](https://cosmosscholars.com/phms/index.php/ijmst/article/download/2341/1490/4319).

**Short-term reversal in India (thin evidence)**
- **Parthasarathy & Sendilvelu** study returns after large monthly price moves in large, liquid Indian stocks to look for short-horizon strategies. Their results were not visible to me. — [CEEOL](https://ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=1054639)
- **Lower-reliability paper:** reports reversals for a 1-1 short-term strategy across winner-loser combinations, and no momentum or reversal for non-overlapping medium- and long-term strategies. — [Scribd paper](https://www.scribd.com/doc/88006578/Essay-1)
- **Broader Indian literature:** short-run momentum followed by long-run reversal (Joshipura; Maheshwari & Dhankar; Tripathi & Aggarwal). — [Publishing India paper](https://www.publishingindia.com/storage/PDFBrochures/2192.pdf); [IJFM](https://www.publishingindia.com/archive/ijfm/a-study-of-contrarian-and-momentum-profits-in-indian-stock-market)

**Technical, breakout and moving-average rules in India**
- **Mitra (2011, Quantitative Finance):** moving-average rules on four Indian indices produced significant gross returns, but the returns could not be captured after bid-ask spreads and brokerage. Short averages trade often, so costs eat into them. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v11y2011i2p287-297.html)
- **Muruganandan (2020)** (Sensex, 2000–2018, bull and bear phases): the RSI rule lost money even before costs. A MACD sell signal beat the unconditional mean in bear markets before costs. Sharpe ratios were weak. — [Colombo Business Journal](https://mgmt.cmb.ac.lk/cbj/index.php/testing-the-profitability-of-technical-trading-rules-across-market-cycles-evidence-from-india/)
- **Mitra (2002)** (crossover and filter rules on 4 stocks, 1995–1999): found excess returns after costs, for an older, less efficient period. — [Indian Journal of Finance 2014 review](https://www.indianjournalofcapitalmarkets.com/index.php/IJF/article/download/71980/58008/125426)

### Inferences
- **Horizon mismatch:** The robust Indian evidence (momentum, 52-week high) is at **monthly to semi-annual** rebalancing and holding periods. That is "positional", not "days to weeks" swing trading. Swing setups coded from the intraday books (Gujral & Vaidya, Aziz) have **no published Indian evidence base** that I found.
- **Gap between paper returns and this account:** Index CAGR figures are gross of retail costs. A concentrated 5–10-stock momentum or 52-week-high portfolio on ₹20,000 costs about 0.6–1.0% per round trip per name (section 3). Annual cost is roughly monthly turnover × that round-trip cost × 12:
  - Monthly rebalancing with 25–50% turnover costs about **2–6% a year**, comparable to the index's excess return of about 5 points (18.8% vs 13.9%). It could consume most of the premium.
  - Semi-annual rebalancing, as in the NSE index, with about 50% turnover twice a year costs about 0.6–1.0% a year.
  - A 30-name copy (about 2.5% per round trip) or rapid swing trading of the same signals would consume the premium.
  Slower rebalancing is therefore much better suited to this account.
- **Positional drawdowns versus the trial stop:** Momentum's historical drawdowns of 34–70% at index level mean a concentrated momentum portfolio could plausibly breach the trial's ₹2,000 (10%) drawdown stop in an ordinary momentum crash or correction. The 10R stop is tighter than the strategy's normal drawdowns. The 1-year return to June 2026 was negative.
- **Signals worth testing as swing entries with daily data:**
  - 52-week-high proximity or breakout within a momentum-ranked universe.
  - Post-earnings drift after large negative or positive surprises, where the Indian evidence is strongest for bad-news drift lasting about a week or more.
  - Simple trend or regime filters, such as an index above a moving average.
  Pure chart breakouts and oscillator rules have the weakest support.
- **Cheaper route to the momentum premium:** Momentum index funds or ETFs capture the evidence-backed premium at low cost. That is investing, though, not a test of the trader's skill or tool.

### Gaps
- I could not access the Nifty200 Momentum 30 factsheet or methodology (niftyindices.com was blocked), so **index turnover and an authoritative maximum drawdown, including the 2024–25 fall from the September 2024 peak, are unverified**. Search budget ran out before a dedicated check.
- IIMA WML factor statistics (mean, volatility, drawdowns) and Ansari & Khan's results could not be retrieved.
- I found no Indian study that measures swing-horizon (2–20 day) strategy returns **net of current delivery costs** (0.1% STT each side plus DP). I also found no recent large-sample Indian PEAD study using standardized unexpected earnings.

## 5. Screen time and testability on historical data

### Takeaway
- **Intraday** needs the whole 9:15–15:30 session, 375 minutes. It can be tested only on minute data, which Kite serves in 60-day chunks from about 2015, and results are very sensitive to fill assumptions relative to tight stops.
- **Swing and positional** trading can run on end-of-day data, roughly 30–60 minutes after the close. It can be backtested on long daily histories of up to 2,000 days per Kite request, and fill assumptions matter about 10 times less.
- **F&O options** are the hardest to test, because Kite gives **no historical data for expired options**.

### Cited Findings
- **Kite Connect historical data limits per request:** minute data 60 days; 3, 5 and 10-minute data 100 days; 15 and 30-minute data 200 days; 60-minute data 400 days; daily data 2,000 days. — [Medium guide to Kite historical API](https://medium.com/@algotrading0103/extracting-historical-data-through-kites-historical-api-for-algorithmic-trading-6b1a82e1e293); 200-day figure for 15-minute data confirmed on [Kite forum](https://kite.trade/forum/discussion/16124/historical-15-minute-ohlc-data-availability-pricing-and-api-limits-for-equity-stocks)
- **Rate limits and cost:** Up to 3 requests per second, no daily or monthly request cap, and historical data included in the Kite Connect subscription. — [Kite forum](https://kite.trade/forum/discussion/16124/historical-15-minute-ohlc-data-availability-pricing-and-api-limits-for-equity-stocks)
- **Expired contracts:** Historical data for **expired options is unavailable**. Expired futures are available only as daily candles through continuous data. Minute data is available from about 2015, subject to the instrument. — [Kite forum](https://kite.trade/forum/discussion/16124/historical-15-minute-ohlc-data-availability-pricing-and-api-limits-for-equity-stocks); [Kite forum, historic futures data](https://kite.trade/forum/discussion/1587/historic-futures-data)

### Inferences
- **Screen time:**
  - Intraday MIS: the full session plus preparation, about 6.5–7 hours a day. Manual order entry for opening-range or VWAP setups needs constant attention.
  - Swing CNC: an end-of-day scan after 15:30 plus order placement the next morning, about 30–60 minutes a day. This is my estimate, not sourced. Intraday monitoring is optional if stops are held as resting orders.
  - Option buying: the full session, with the fastest-moving instrument.
  The trader can watch the whole session, so screen time does not rule anything out. It does show that intraday ties the trader's time to the tool's flag rate.
- **Testability:**
  - Swing on daily data allows thousands of historical trades: 15 or more years across 200–500 NSE stocks. Next-open or close fills are simple, and a 0.05% fill error costs only about 0.02R on a 5% stop.
  - Intraday backtests need minute bars stitched from 60-day requests. Real intraday fills, including stop slippage, queue position and the opening auction, are not in OHLC bars, and the same 0.05% error costs about 0.2R on a 0.5% stop. A daily-bar backtest is therefore far more trustworthy than a minute-bar one at this account's risk sizes.
  - Options strategies cannot be backtested from Kite at all, because expired contracts are missing. They would need a paid vendor or NSE end-of-day bhavcopies.
  - Survivorship bias (delisted stocks) affects both equity modes and needs a point-in-time universe. The non-peer-reviewed NSE momentum backtest above explicitly corrects for it.

### Gaps
- Official Kite Connect documentation (kite.trade/docs) was blocked, so the per-interval limits come from a third-party guide and forum replies.
- I could not verify Zerodha GTT order details (stop-loss orders for delivery holdings, validity period), which bear on swing screen time. Search budget ran out.

## 6. Trade frequency and how long statistical confidence takes (including the 10R trial stop)

### Takeaway
Detecting a realistic edge of +0.1–0.25R per trade with t ≈ 2 takes **hundreds of trades**, given a per-trade standard deviation of about 1.0–1.5R. After costs it often takes thousands, because costs shrink the edge more than the noise.
- Intraday piles up trades about 4 times faster (20–40 a month) than swing (about 6–12 a month) or positional (about 2–4), but its costs of about 0.2R a trade can wipe out the very edge being measured.
- The trial's 10R drawdown stop is a **risk budget, not a statistical test**. With a genuinely positive edge it still has a high chance of ending the trial early.

### Cited Findings
- Cost inputs come from section 3. Base-rate inputs: 71% of intraday traders lose and 80% of those with more than 500 trades a year lose ([SEBI intraday study](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/jul-2024/1721818140715.pdf)); 87.7% of F&O traders lose ([SEBI Aug 2026](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/aug-2026/1787233506209.pdf)).
- I found no published Indian source on realistic trade counts per holding period. The frequencies below are assumptions.

### Inferences
**Assumed frequencies:**
- **Intraday:** 1–2 trades a day, so 20–40 a month. More than 2 a day puts the trader in SEBI's 500+ trades a year group, where 80% lose.
- **Swing CNC with 5% stops:** at most 5 concurrent ₹4,000 slots. Throughput is about slots × 21 trading days ÷ average holding days: about 21 a month at a 5-day hold, about 10 at 10 days, and about 5 at 20 days. A realistic 6–12 a month.
- **Positional (monthly momentum or 52-week high):** about 2–5 trades a month.

**Sample size:** I used n = (t × SD / E)² with two-outcome trades (win = +b R, loss = −1R) and the per-trade costs from section 3.

- **Implication:** Swing's slow live sample is best handled by first testing the rules on 10+ years of daily history, where thousands of trades are available. The paper-trading of every flag then confirms the backtest, rather than having to prove the edge from scratch. Intraday's fast sample is real, but only matters if the gross edge clearly exceeds about 0.2–0.4R plus slippage.
- **10R trial stop (Monte Carlo, 20,000 paths, independent trades, no slippage):**
  - **Cumulative −₹2,000 from the start, within 100 trades:**
    - 50% win, 1.5R system: swing 13%, intraday 30%, options 57%.
    - 40% win, 2R system: swing 30%, intraday 49%, options 72%.
    - No gross edge: swing 75%, intraday 89%, options 97%.
  - **Peak-to-trough 10R drawdown, within 100 trades:** 43–73% for the swing cases and 65–86% for the intraday cases, even though the swing cases have positive net expectancy.
  - Interpretation: if the stop means a ₹2,000 loss from the starting capital, it will usually stop a no-edge system and seldom stop a decent-edge swing system. If it means a drawdown from peak, it will often end even a winning system. **This should be defined explicitly in the trial plan.**
- **Costs alone with zero gross edge:** a 10R loss is expected after about **48 intraday trades** (0.21R each, about 1–2 months at 1–2 a day), about **83 swing trades** (0.12R each, about 8–14 months), or about **32 option trades** (0.31R each).

### Gaps
- No Indian data on realistic trade counts or per-trade R distributions for retail setups. The figures above are modelled assumptions, and real outcomes have fatter tails and serial correlation.
- Correction for testing many setups at once was not modelled beyond showing the stricter t = 3 hurdle.

## 7. What distinguishes the profitable minority of Indian retail traders

### Takeaway
In Indian data, the profitable minority tends to:
- **trade less often**
- **be older**
- **use futures or the cash market rather than bought options**
- **trade derivatives with a turnover small relative to their capital, and keep a cash-equity presence**

Experience by itself does **not** help in F&O: new and regular traders lose at almost identical rates. Losses persist, and only 0.5% of continuous F&O traders were profitable in all five years from FY22 to FY26. For cash-equity investors (not traders), experience goes with lower turnover and better returns.

### Inferences
- The traits that go with better outcomes (fewer trades, longer horizons, linear instruments, a cash-equity base) all point **away from high-frequency intraday trading and option buying**, and toward lower-turnover delivery trading. Indian regulators, however, publish **no profitability study for delivery (CNC) swing traders**. That conclusion rests on indirect evidence, not a direct measurement.
- The persistence data argues for a hard **stop-loss on the experiment itself**, which this trader's 10R rule already provides. Most F&O losers keep trading after two losing years, and 90–92% of them lose again.

### Gaps
- No SEBI or NSE study reports the share of profitable **delivery or swing** traders, or profitability by holding period within the cash segment. Kamath reports no profitability share for intraday or delivery traders, and says MTF is too new.
- The Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean Taiwan day-trader result (fewer than 1% predictably profitable) could not be verified from search results, so it is not cited.

## 8. Comparison table and which holding period to test first

### Takeaway
On every measure available for this exact account (base rates, cost per trade in R, feasibility at ₹200 risk, diversification, backtest reliability), **swing or positional equity using CNC delivery, with daily-bar signals, should be tested first**. F&O is effectively infeasible and has the worst base rates. Intraday is feasible, but has a 71–80% base rate of losing and costs that, at tight stops, can equal or exceed a typical retail edge. Its main advantage is fast sample accumulation, which makes it a good candidate for **paper-only** testing in parallel.

### Inferences
**Evidence for testing swing/positional CNC first:**
1. It is the only mode where costs at ₹200 risk (about 0.10–0.15R) are clearly below a plausible gross edge (about 0.2–0.3R), and where costs do not grow with trade count to multiples of the account each year.
2. Its signals (momentum rank, 52-week-high proximity, post-earnings drift, regime filter) have Indian evidence. The supporting evidence is at monthly or longer horizons, so hold periods of a few weeks, not a few days, fit the evidence better.
3. It can be backtested on long daily histories with fill error that hardly matters. The slow live sample can therefore build on a statistically meaningful backtest instead of waiting 2–3 years.
4. The traits SEBI links with lower loss rates (lower frequency, cash-market presence, no option buying) point this way.

**Intraday as a paper-only parallel track:**
- Keep intraday flags (Gujral & Vaidya and Aziz setups) on **paper only** until a few hundred flags show net expectancy above about 0.25R after modelled costs of 0.2–0.4R plus slippage.
- Prefer wider intraday stops (≥ 0.75–1%) to keep costs at or below about 0.14R.
- Cap frequency well below SEBI's 500-trades-a-year group.

**Do not test F&O with this account:** it is infeasible at ₹200 risk and has the worst base rates and highest costs. The 2024–26 rule changes raised the capital needed further.

**Design caveat for the trial:** Define whether the 10R stop is measured from the starting capital or from the equity peak. With realistic edges, a peak-based 10R stop will often end even a winning swing system within 100 trades (43–73% in the model above).

### Gaps
- The recommendation rests on indirect evidence for swing and delivery trading. No Indian regulator or broker has published profitability for delivery swing traders by holding period.
- Several figures are from secondary coverage, because primary SEBI, NSE and Zerodha pages could not be fetched and the search budget ran out. Not verified: Zerodha MIS leverage and square-off time, GTT details, Nifty200 Momentum 30 turnover and its 2024–25 drawdown, and any post-2024 SEBI intraday update.
